The Best Florida Beach Towns for Old Florida Charm Are Quiet Because They're Far

Shrimp boats, fish camps and river towns with no high rises. Old Florida is the quietest, most affordable coast left, and the farthest from an airport.

By Mark and Betsy ScottNov 3, 20266 min read

In Apalachicola you can walk to breakfast past the shrimp boats. The storefronts are brick, from the town's cotton and oyster days, and nobody is in a hurry. At night you watch the sun go down over the bay.

Then you look up how far it is to the airport. About an hour and a half to Tallahassee.

That's the deal Old Florida offers, and it's a good one if you go in knowing it. We lived in Tallahassee before we moved to the beach, and the old river and bay towns south of it are a different Florida. So we checked what they have in common.

The quiet has a price, and it isn't money

We score 20 towns as Old Florida: fishing villages and river towns where the boat ramp matters more than the mall. They're the quietest towns on the coast, the closest to the water and among the most affordable.

They're also the farthest from everything. Only 3 of the 20 score a 4 or better for airport access, against 62% of the rest of the coast. Groceries, hospitals and restaurants follow the same pattern.

The towns where Old Florida survived are the ones the highways skipped.

We ranked them on peace and quiet, life on the water and affordability.

Apalachicola is where we'd start. Shrimp boats in the harbor, a walkable old downtown, and the quiet beach on St. George Island just across the bridge. Low areas take storm surge, and big city services are far off.

Cedar Key is a tiny island town of clam farms and kayaks. You paddle the backwater keys in the morning and eat clams on Dock Street at sunset. Recent hurricanes hit it hard, and the nearest full grocery store is about 40 minutes away.

Chokoloskee is a shell mound island at the edge of Everglades National Park, the last stop before the Ten Thousand Islands. A good day means poling a skiff through the mangroves for snook and stopping at the old Smallwood Store on the way in. It's extremely remote and very low, and the mosquitoes are part of the deal.

Steinhatchee is a river fishing village where summer means scallops. You run the river to the grass flats at dawn and clean your catch on the dock. Idalia hit in 2023 and Helene in 2024.

Yankeetown is an old river town of live oaks and fish camps on the Withlacoochee. Launch a kayak at Bird Creek Park and climb the tower at the 413 acre Withlacoochee Gulf Preserve. Crystal River is close for errands, and Inglis up the river is even quieter. Be careful here: the whole town is in the flood zone, Helene pushed about 12 feet of surge into nearly all of it in 2024, and few owners carry flood insurance.

Horseshoe Beach is a fishing town at the end of the road in Dixie County, with canals, a little Main Street and a Labor Day parade. It's also had the hardest few years of any town on this list. Idalia, Debby and Helene all hit within 13 months, and the mayor estimated about 175 of 365 homes were lost after Helene.

Everglades City is stone crab country and the way into the Ten Thousand Islands. The national park's Marjory Stoneman Douglas Visitor Center opened in December 2025 with boat tours and a kayak launch. Nearly every property is in a flood zone, and the hospital is 35 to 45 minutes away.

St. Marks is the exception on airports: about 30 minutes from Tallahassee's. The paved trail from Tallahassee runs sixteen miles of old railroad right into town, and the wildlife refuge next door has more than 86,000 acres and a lighthouse rebuilt in 1842. Storm surge has put water in town five times since 2005.

Ozello is a handful of homes on marsh islands at the end of a nine mile road through salt marsh. You launch a kayak from the back yard and may not see another boat all morning. There are almost no services and one road in and out.

Also carrying the style: Carrabelle, Suwannee, Keaton Beach, Matlacha, Port St. Joe, Cortez, Tarpon Springs and Islamorada. Our earlier story, Old Florida Is Still Out There, tells a few of their stories.

Cheap to buy, not always cheap to own

Typical prices in our top nine run from about $216K in Horseshoe Beach to about $342K in Apalachicola, well below the famous coast. The bigger number is often insurance. Low ground means flood coverage, elevation rules and sometimes a rebuild, and nearly every town here has taken storm surge in the last decade. What nobody tells you about Florida home insurance and the six bills nobody puts on the listing walk through it.

Before you fall for a porch

  • Ask for the elevation certificate and the flood history, and what was repaired after the last storm.
  • Get real insurance quotes before an offer, not after.
  • Drive the errands. Time the trip to groceries, a hospital and the airport you'll actually use.
  • Stay a week off season. How to test drive a town has a plan.
  • Read the hurricane season checklist.

Old Florida is still out there. It just never moved closer to the airport.
