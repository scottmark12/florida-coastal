Everyone Expected the Newcomers to Turn Florida Blue. They Did the Opposite.

In Tuesday's election, the counties gaining the most people from other states barely moved. What the results say about who is moving to Florida, and what matters more when you choose a town.

By Mark ScottNov 6, 20266 min read

In The Villages, the sprawling retirement community northwest of Orlando, almost everyone is from somewhere else. Since 2020, arrivals from other states have added the equivalent of nearly 30 percent of the population of Sumter County, which surrounds it, more than any other county in Florida. Its median age is 68.

On Tuesday, as the country swung hard toward Democrats, Sumter was the only county in Florida that moved the other way.

If you are thinking about moving to Florida, you are in crowded company, and for years the assumption was that all those newcomers would eventually make the state more Democratic. Tuesday's election was the clearest test yet. It showed the opposite.

Here is what the results say about who is moving to Florida, and what matters more when you are choosing a town.

A blue wave that Florida mostly rode out

Democrats had a strong night nationally, winning control of both chambers of Congress. The next morning, Gov. Ron DeSantis called it "close to an extinction-level event for Republicans in many parts of the country."

Florida moved left too, but it did not flip. Byron Donalds, the Republican, beat David Jolly for governor 50.0 percent to 48.7 percent, according to The Associated Press, and Senator Ashley Moody held her seat.

Compared with Donald J. Trump's 13 point win in 2024, the governor's race moved 11.7 points toward Democrats. That sounds like a lot, until you see the rest of the country. Of 33 governor's races with a Republican facing a Democrat, Florida's shift ranked 17th, right on the national median. Texas swung 21 points. Pennsylvania swung 24.

Newcomers voted Republican

If newcomers had pushed Florida left, the counties that gained the most of them would have swung the hardest. On the chart below, each dot is a county. Further right means more newcomers since 2020. Higher means a bigger swing toward Democrats. The dots slope down.

Sumter County gained more newcomers than any other county and was the only one in Florida that moved toward Republicans. Charlotte, Walton, Flagler and St. Johns, all among the top destinations, swung far less than the national average. The biggest swings came from Miami-Dade, Broward and Orange Counties, which lost more residents to other states than they gained.

The county that gained the most newcomers was the only one in Florida that moved right.

The NBC News exit poll shows the same thing voter by voter.

Voter rolls agree. In a study in Political Science Quarterly, Daniel A. Smith of the University of Florida found that of the people 25 and older who moved to Florida and registered to vote in 2024, 48 percent registered as Republicans and 23 percent as Democrats. Transplants from nearly every state, blue states included, leaned Republican.

Who moves here matters more than where they come from

The bigger pattern is age. Florida's top destinations for newcomers are retirement and semi retirement communities, and the older a county is, the less it moved on Tuesday.

Sumter is 57.6 percent age 65 and up. Charlotte, Sarasota, Citrus, Collier and Flagler are each a third to two fifths seniors, about double metro Orlando or Tampa, and all swung less than half the national average. Once age is taken into account, where people moved from stops mattering at all.

Where the big swings came from

The counties that moved hardest toward Democrats were younger, working age places, including Osceola, Orange and Polk along the Interstate 4 corridor, Heartland farm counties like Hendry and Hardee, and Miami-Dade and Broward.

Osceola County, south of Orlando, is the clearest example. Two years ago Mr. Trump carried it by 1.5 points. On Tuesday Mr. Jolly won it by 17.8.

Across all 67 counties, the share of Hispanic residents was the single strongest predictor of how far a county swung. But Hispanic voters did not move as one bloc. Puerto Rican voters backed Mr. Jolly 68 percent to 30 percent and other non-Cuban Hispanic voters favored him 59 to 40, while Cuban American voters backed Mr. Donalds 58 to 42, according to the exit poll. In Miami-Dade, which went to Mr. Jolly, a Miami Herald analysis found that Hispanic majority precincts still leaned Republican, just by much less than in 2022.

Put it together and just two things, a county's share of residents 65 and older and its Hispanic share, explain about four fifths of the difference in how Florida's counties swung.

The newcomers did not turn Florida blue. The older ones built the cushion that kept it red.

What this means if you're moving here

Statewide, Florida looks likely to stay Republican for a while, and the people moving here are part of the reason. But the statewide map tells you very little about daily life in a particular town.

The decisions that shape what it costs to own a home and what a place feels like, like property tax rates, zoning, growth and many flood and storm rules, are made by county commissions and city councils. Those are worth more of your attention than how a county voted for governor.

Before you choose a town

  • Local government. Look up when the next city and county elections are, and skim a few recent commission or council agendas. You will learn fast what the town is arguing about.
  • Property taxes. Rates vary a lot by county and city. The bills not on the listing walks through what to ask.
  • Insurance and storms. Get quotes before you make an offer. Start with our insurance guide and hurricane season guide.
  • Flood risk. Check the street, not just the town. See our flood rankings for 50 beach towns.
  • Try it first. Spend a week there in the off season. How to test drive a town has a plan.

A county's election results describe how its voters leaned on one day. They say nothing about who your neighbors will be or whether you will feel at home, and Florida Coastal does not use them to score or recommend towns.

About this analysis

A county's swing is the change in the Democratic share of the two party margin, from the 2024 presidential race to the 2026 governor's race. Newcomers are net arrivals from other U.S. states between July 2020 and July 2025, from U.S. Census Bureau Vintage 2025 estimates, as a share of each county's 2020 population. Results are unofficial, as of Nov. 5 and 6, 2026.

Across the 67 counties, the swing was correlated with newcomers (r = −0.44), the share of residents 65 and older (r = −0.63) and Hispanic share (r = +0.75), all with p < 0.001. In a model that includes age, migration is not significant (p = 0.53). Age and Hispanic share together explain 79 percent of the variation between counties (R² = 0.789). County comparisons describe places, not individual voters, so they were checked against NBC News exit poll results.

This analysis describes election results. It is not a guide to where any group of people lives or should live. Florida Coastal follows federal, state and local fair housing laws.
