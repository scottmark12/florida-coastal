Forget Kansas. Florida Is the Flat One.

Florida is flatter than Kansas, about as flat as Holland, and home to 23 million people. This fall it flooded two different ways in one week. Here's why, and what to check before you buy.

Hidden Florida·By Florida Coastal·Nov 3, 2026·5 min read

On a sunny Tuesday in late October, seawater spilled over the seawalls along Collins Avenue in Miami Beach. There was no storm. It was the highest King Tide on record in South Florida, and it kept Miami's streets wet for more than 11 days straight.

A week later, farther up the coast, residents of Palm Bay were paddling kayaks down their own streets. About 20 inches of rain fell there in two days, more than Los Angeles sees in a typical year. Mayor Rob Medina said the storm "devastated our community."

Two floods, two causes, one week. What connects them is something few people consider when they picture Florida: it is astonishingly flat.

Flatter than Kansas

Ask Americans to name the flattest state and most will say Kansas. The average elevation in Kansas is about 2,000 feet. In Florida it is about 100. The state's highest point, Britton Hill in the Panhandle, stands 345 feet, shorter than a football field is long. Plenty of Miami condo towers are taller.

Abroad, the comparison gets stranger. The only countries flatter on average are tiny island nations like the Maldives and city states like Singapore. The Netherlands is roughly as flat as Florida, and a quarter its size.

The reason lies underground. For millions of years, Florida sat beneath a warm, shallow sea, where shells, coral and sediment hardened into limestone. That old seafloor is now the ground under 23 million people. It is porous, like a hard sponge, and holds the Floridan Aquifer, the source of most of the state's drinking water. It also lets seawater seep up from below, which is why a seawall alone cannot keep the ocean out of Miami.

Two ways to flood

On flat land, a few extra feet of water travels a long way. In Marathon, in the middle of the Keys, a tide three feet above normal would put more than half the town under water. In Miami Beach, it would be about 40 percent. That is the threat from King Tides and hurricane storm surge.

Palm Bay faces a different problem. Only about 0.2 percent of the city sits low enough for even a three foot tide to reach. But flat ground is poor at shedding rain. Water that would run downhill elsewhere sits until canals and creeks carry it off, and much of Palm Bay was built on drained wetlands that empty slowly through Turkey Creek into the Indian River Lagoon. When 20 inches arrive in two days, the system cannot keep up. It happened there on a smaller scale in 2023, too.

A town can be safe from one kind of flood and exposed to the other. In recent years, Florida's major floods have split almost evenly between the two.

Flat doesn't have to mean flooded

America has been here before. About half of New Orleans sits below sea level, and after its levees failed during Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the country spent about $14.5 billion on stronger levees, floodwalls and pumps. The Dutch rebuilt after a 1953 North Sea flood killed more than 1,800 people, and later began giving rivers room to spill onto land set aside for it.

Flat places can be safe places when water is planned for. Some Florida towns are raising roads, adding pumps and fitting storm drains with valves that stop the tide from pushing back up. For anyone choosing where to live, it is worth asking which towns are doing that work.

The question matters because the moves keep coming. State economists expect Florida to add roughly 875 residents a day this year, and the largest group arriving from other states is people in their 50s and 60s. Flatness is no reason to skip Florida. It is a reason to choose the town, and the street, with care.

In a state whose highest point is shorter than a football field, the few feet under your house matter more than you think.

Before you buy

  • Tide map. NOAA's free Sea Level Rise Viewer shows which streets flood at 1, 2 and 3 feet above high tide.
  • FEMA zone. The Flood Map Service Center shows whether a lender will require flood insurance.
  • Rain history. Neither map shows rain flooding. Ask the neighbors, and drive by after a heavy storm.
  • Insurance. Standard homeowners policies do not cover floods, and flood policies usually take 30 days to start. Our insurance guide has more.
  • The house. Ask for an elevation certificate and check how high the first floor sits. Our hurricane guide covers the rest.
