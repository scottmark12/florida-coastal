Florida Beach Towns Ranked by Flood Risk: The 15 Riskiest Are All Islands

We ranked 50 Florida beach towns from 1 to 10 on tide flooding and FEMA's flood map. Anna Maria is the riskiest, Stuart the safest, and every one of the top 15 sits on an island.

By Mark and Betsy ScottNov 3, 20268 min read

Siesta Key is a short drive and one bridge from downtown Sarasota. Same bay, same storms, same sunsets.

On our flood list, Siesta Key scores a 7.6 out of 10. Sarasota scores a 3.7.

We ranked 50 of the beach towns we cover on two public maps: how much of each town the ocean reaches at a high tide, and how much sits in FEMA's high risk flood zone. 1 is the lowest risk, 10 the highest. Anna Maria is the riskiest at 9.9, then Marathon, Sanibel and Fort Myers Beach. Stuart is the safest at 1.5.

No single town surprised us as much as the pattern did. Every one of the 15 riskiest towns sits on an island.

The top 15 are all islands

Barrier islands and keys, every one: Anna Maria, Marathon, Sanibel, Fort Myers Beach, Islamorada, Bradenton Beach, Miami Beach, Marco Island, Key West, Treasure Island, Cedar Key, St. Pete Beach, Navarre Beach, Siesta Key and Longboat Key. Most have a median elevation of 2 to 5 feet, and nearly all of their land is in FEMA's high risk zone.

Cross the bridge and the number drops. Palm Beach scores a 6.4. West Palm Beach, across the lagoon, scores a 3.1. On the Emerald Coast, Navarre Beach and Pensacola Beach come in at 7.9 and 7.1, and Fort Walton Beach, on the mainland side of the sound, is the second safest town on the list at 1.9.

It isn't a perfect rule. Satellite Beach is on a barrier island and scores a 2.7, because very little of it sits in FEMA's zone. But if you're choosing between the island and the town across from it, the island almost always carries more risk.

And most of them are on the Gulf

Ten of the 15 Gulf Coast towns on our list score a 7 or higher. On the Atlantic, it's 2 of 24.

Part of it is the water itself. The Gulf off Florida's west coast is wide and shallow for miles out, and that lets a storm push the sea up onto the land. The big storms of the last few years show it. Ian in 2022, Idalia in 2023, Helene and Milton in 2024 all hit the Gulf side.

The Gulf towns that score under 7 are the bigger cities: Naples, Cape Coral, Sarasota, Clearwater and Venice. The Atlantic's two exceptions are Miami Beach, which floods on sunny days every fall when the king tides come in, and St. Augustine, whose old downtown flooded in back to back hurricanes in 2016 and 2017.

Ten of the 15 Gulf towns we score land at 7 or higher. On the Atlantic, two of 24 do.

A middle score isn't a promise

Mexico Beach scores a 4.4, right in the middle of the list. In October 2018, Hurricane Michael came ashore there as a Category 5 and largely destroyed the town.

We've ridden out several hurricanes on this coast, Michael included. For us that usually meant a week or more without power and a few trees down, nothing worse. Our score can't tell you which of those two stories you'd get. It measures how much of a town sits low and inside FEMA's zone, not what the worst storm in 50 years will do.

It doesn't count rain either. Palm Bay is the fifth safest town on the list, and on November 2 people were paddling kayaks down its streets after up to two feet of rain in two days. We wrote about both kinds of flooding this fall.

How we score it

The score compares each town to the other 49 on this list. It's a ranking, not a prediction about any one house. We rank every town on how much of its land is under water at 3 feet above a normal high tide, rank it again on how much is in FEMA's high risk zone, average the two with equal weight and scale it from 1 to 10. Median elevation is shown for each town, but it isn't part of the score.

Our own stretch: Santa Rosa Beach (30A)

7.4% of its land is under water at 3 feet, the 38.8th percentile. 53.5% is in FEMA's high risk zone, the 55.1st percentile. The average is 46.95, and 1 + 9 × 0.4695 = 5.2. Rank 29 of 50, right in the middle. We'd rather tell you that than leave it out.

Before you fall for a house

  • Look up the street, not the town. NOAA's free Sea Level Rise Viewer shows which streets flood at 1, 2 and 3 feet.
  • Check the FEMA zone for the exact address in the Flood Map Service Center.
  • Ask the neighbors whether the street flooded in the last few storms, rain included.
  • Get a flood quote before you make an offer. Standard homeowners policies don't cover floods. Our insurance guide has more.
  • Look at the house itself. A newer elevated home, or a condo above the ground floor, can change the picture in a high scoring town.
