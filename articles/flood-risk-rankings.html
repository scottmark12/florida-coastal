Florida Beach Towns Ranked by Flood Risk

How to read it: every town gets a flood score from 1 to 10. 1 is the lowest risk, 10 is the highest. The score mixes two things: how much of the town the ocean reaches at a high tide, and how much is in FEMA’s high risk flood zone.

What stands out

  • Highest risk: Anna Maria (9.9), Marathon (9.7), Sanibel (9.3), Fort Myers Beach (9.0). All four sit almost entirely in FEMA's high risk zone, and big shares of their land are within three feet of high tide.
  • Lowest risk: Stuart (1.5), Fort Walton Beach (1.9), Melbourne (2.1), Jupiter (2.2), Palm Bay (2.3).
  • Marathon floods first. A quarter of its land is under water at just one foot above normal high tide, and 58% at three feet.
  • Treasure Island is 99.9% inside FEMA's high risk zone, but only 19% of its land is below three feet. FEMA's map also covers storm surge and waves, not just tide.
  • Palm Bay ranks among the safest, and it still flooded badly on November 2, 2026. That was rain, which neither map measures.

How the Combined Score works

The score compares each town to the other 49 on this list. It is a ranking, not a prediction of whether a particular home will flood.

  1. Rank on tide flooding. Each town gets a percentile from 0 to 100 based on how much of its land is under water at three feet above normal high tide.
  2. Rank on the FEMA map. Each town gets a second percentile from 0 to 100 based on how much of its land is in FEMA's high risk zone. Towns that tie share a percentile.
  3. Average and scale. The two percentiles are averaged with equal weight and scaled from 1 to 10.

Worked example: Santa Rosa Beach (30A)

7.4% of its land is under water at three feet, which puts it at the 38.8th percentile. 53.5% is in FEMA's high risk zone, the 55.1st percentile. The average is 46.95, and 1 + 9 × 0.4695 = 5.2. It lands right in the middle of the list, at rank 29.

Median elevation is shown for each town for information only. It is not part of the score.

What neither map shows: rain

Both maps are about the ocean and official flood zones. Neither counts heavy rain, which is how Palm Bay ended up with kayaks in the streets after up to two feet of rain in two days. A low score does not mean a street never floods in a downpour. Read the full story of Florida's two floods this fall.

Before you buy, check the street

  • Tide map. NOAA's free Sea Level Rise Viewer shows the exact streets that flood at 1, 2 and 3 feet.
  • FEMA zone. Look up the address in the Flood Map Service Center.
  • Rain history. Ask the neighbors whether the street flooded in recent storms.
  • Insurance. Standard homeowners policies do not cover floods. Get a flood quote before you make an offer. Our insurance guide has more.
